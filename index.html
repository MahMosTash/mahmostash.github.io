01 · questions

There are people behind the data.

I like questions more than answers. A better prediction or a metric moving up isn't always enough. I want to understand the decisions people make, not just measure their actions. These are the questions that connect most of what I do.

  1. Q1

    When a system makes decisions, how do people respond?

    An incentive can get a driver to complete more rides today. But is it improving their experience, or just encouraging short-term behavior that won't last? I care about what happens after the metric moves.

    → incentive work at Snapp → play the bandit

  2. Q2

    Can AI help people decide better, not just predict better?

    AI tools are usually judged by accuracy. The real test is whether the person using them makes a better call. That's the goal of my next Driver Voice layer: not just flagging a problem, but helping a team decide what to fix.

    → Driver Voice, layer 2

  3. Q3

    Can a model learn markets from markets that never existed?

    Financial data is short, noisy, and keeps changing. If we pretrain only on simulated processes, which kinds of synthetic "economies" teach a model something real?

    → synthetic pre-training → generate a fake market

  4. Q4

    What do economic shocks do to the choices families make?

    Inflation shows up in prices, but also in decisions like whether a child stays in school. I like using data to trace macro shocks down to individual behavior.

    → inflation & school attendance

04 · teaching

The part I'd do for free

One semester, students told us the practical homework wasn't interesting enough. Instead of patching it, I sat down and designed a completely new project where students competed against each other.

I care a lot about students being genuinely interested in what they learn. Interest is what keeps me learning, so I try to build it on purpose: assignments that look like real problems, a bit of competition, and simulations where the wrong intuition fails loudly.

As head TA I spend as much time on the system around the course as on the content: problem sets that teach instead of filter, and a TA team that treats office hours as the most important hour of the week.

Read the crowd.

You and 99 bots play 15 rounds in a small casino. Every payoff depends on how many others choose the same game, so yesterday's average is a bad guide. The bots have personalities: herders follow the crowd, contrarians avoid it, and some chase past averages. One bot models all of them. Can you beat it?

07 · about

A bit more about me

I've been into trading since high school. Trying to understand why prices move is what first got me reading economics, and eventually led to my Economics minor. Over time the question shifted from the market to the people in it: why they decide the way they do, and what changes their minds.

I never wanted to learn something just to pass an exam. I want to see what I can actually do with it. That's why I keep one foot in research, one in industry, and both in the classroom.
